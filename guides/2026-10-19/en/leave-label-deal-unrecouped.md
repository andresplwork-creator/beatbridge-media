Yes, you can often leave a label deal even if you have not recouped your advance. An unrecouped balance is not a debt you repay from your own pocket: it is an account the label recovers from your royalties. If someone pays that balance, or a negotiated amount, you can become independent again and take back control of your catalog. In many cases, depending on the contract and what your songs earn, there is even cash left over for you.

This guide explains why you see so little money, why a label might accept an exit and how to prepare one step by step.

## Why you see almost no money

Your royalty share is usually a small percentage of what the label earns from your music. An illustrative example:

- You received a 50,000 advance and the label charged another 30,000 in recoupable recording and video costs. Your balance is 80,000.
- Your share is 20% of what the label earns from your recordings.
- If the label earns 100,000 a year from your music, 20,000 a year is credited to your account.
- You would need four years to recoup, as long as no new costs are added and no other album is cross-collateralized.

Meanwhile the label keeps the full 100,000 every year. That is why some artists whose songs earn real money still receive nothing. Read [what unrecouped means](/insights/what-does-unrecouped-mean) for the full mechanics.

## Why a label might accept an exit

For the label, your unrecouped balance is money it expects to recover slowly, with no certainty about when. A serious proposal offers something different: a fixed amount today. Depending on the case, that can be more attractive than waiting, especially if your releases are no longer a priority for the label's team or the contract is close to its end.

There is no guarantee the label will agree. It depends on the contract, the balance, what your recordings earn and the label's strategy. But many artists never raise it because they do not know it is possible.

## How a financed exit works

A funder pays the label or distributor the negotiated amount to close your balance. In return, for an agreed period, it receives a share of the income from those recordings or other rights agreed in the deal. You leave the contract, become independent again and, for that period, own 100% of your catalog. If the catalog earns enough, the deal can also leave you with cash.

## The plan, step by step

1. **Gather the paperwork.** The signed contract with every amendment and side letter, your statements for the last two or three years and any correspondence about options, delivery or releases.
2. **Work out your real balance.** Check which costs were charged, whether albums are cross-collateralized and whether any charge looks wrong. Sometimes an audit reduces the balance.
3. **Find out what your recordings earn.** Not what reaches you, but what the label receives. That figure decides whether an exit is viable and how much a funder can offer.
4. **Separate what the label does not control.** Your songwriter royalties are paid by your collecting society or publisher, not your label, unless you signed a 360 or publishing deal with it. That income can help the deal.
5. **Prepare a concrete proposal.** An amount, a date and what happens to the masters and any pending releases. A proposal with numbers works better than asking to be released.
6. **Close and move your catalog.** Sign, move your releases to the new distributor without losing their ISRCs or streaming history, and update payment instructions.

## What changes for you

| | Before | After a financed exit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Royalties from those recordings | Go to cover the balance, so you are not paid | Shared with the funder for the agreed period, then yours |
| Control of the catalog | The label decides | You decide |
| New music | Tied to the label if it holds options on future albums | Free to release as you choose |
| Cash today | None | Depending on the case, there may be cash for you |

## When it fits and when it does not

It usually fits when your recordings earn steady income, the balance is reasonable compared with that income and the label is no longer investing heavily in your music.

It is harder when the balance is very high compared with what the songs earn, when there are ownership disputes or uncleared samples, or when the contract gives the label long options on albums you have not recorded yet.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Simply stopping delivery.** It can give the label grounds to suspend or extend the contract.
- **Signing with someone else before you are out.** A new exclusive deal can conflict with the one you have.
- **Announcing your exit on social media.** It weakens your negotiating position.
- **Negotiating without numbers.** Without your statements and the label's income, it is hard to be taken seriously.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to pay back the advance if I leave?

Usually not from your own pocket, because the advance is recovered from royalties. But leaving early needs an agreement with the label, and that agreement usually includes a negotiated payment, which is what a funder can cover.

### Do I keep my masters?

It depends on what is negotiated. In many contracts the masters recorded during the deal belong to the label. A financed exit can include getting them back or a licence, but it has to be agreed expressly.

### Can I stay with my label and still get money?

Sometimes. If you are happy with your label, you can look at a [royalty advance](/funding-royalty-advance) on income outside the contract, such as your songwriter royalties.

### How long does it take?

Reviewing the contract and statements takes a few weeks. Negotiation depends on the label, and the whole process usually takes from a few weeks to a few months.
